Comparison types\nThe assessment of subjective well-being after loss, as\nany other form of self-judgment, entails comparisons\n(Vlaev et al., 2011). Well-being comparisons encompass social, temporal, counterfactual, and expectation-based standards (Morina, 2021). Social\ncomparison involves evaluating one’s well-being relative to others (Festinger, 1954). In the context of\nbereavement, individuals may frequently evaluate\ntheir well-being in comparison to that of other\nbereaved and non-bereaved individuals. Temporal\ncomparison refers to evaluating one’s current wellbeing relative to one’s past well-being, or one’s anticipated future well-being (Albert, 1977). Bereaved individuals might compare their current well-being to\ntheir well-being before the loss, which may reinforce\nfeelings of stagnation or despair. Counterfactual comparison is defined as comparing one’s current wellbeing against a mentally simulated alternative state\nof well-being (i.e. counter to the facts) (Kahneman &\nMiller, 1986). Bereaved individuals may reflect on\nhow they would be doing if the loss had not occurred,\nwhich can amplify feelings of regret, guilt, or a sense of\n‘what might have been’ (Eisma et al., 2021). Last,\nexpectation-based comparison implies comparing\none’s well-being to certain expectations or self-guides,\nsuch as how one should be doing at a certain stage of\nlife (Higgins, 1996). In the context of a loss, individuals may evaluate their well-being and grief against\ntheir own or societal expectations of how one should\ncope with loss (McLean et al., 2022). If they perceive\nthemselves as failing to meet these expectations, they\nmay experience negative affective and cognitive reactions.\n1.3. Incremental contribution of aversive wellbeing comparisons\nOur results revealed a nuanced role of comparative\nthinking in mental health outcomes following\nbereavement. This role remained evident even\nwhen controlling for worry, rumination, avoidance,\nand childhood adversity. Comparison frequency consistently emerged as an important predictor of distress across models, particularly in relation to\nanxiety, depression, somatic symptoms, and loneliness both cross-sectionally and longitudinally.\nThese symptoms encompass cognitive and emotional\npatterns and may, therefore, be influenced by the\nrepetitive and evaluative nature of comparison frequency. Comparison affective impact also emerged\nas predictor in correlational analyses. Affective impact directly reflects the\npsychological consequences of frequent comparisons\n(Morina, 2021). This underscores the important role of aversive well-being comparisons in shaping distress following bereavement.\n5.3. Comparative thinking and rumination\nIn models with all predictors, comparison frequency\nover the last three weeks as assessed at T1 did not\nsignificantly predict grief reactions longitudinally,\n\n11\n\nwhereas rumination emerged as the most important\nfactor across models. However, comparative frequency at T2 emerged as the only significant variable\nwhen accounting for autoregressive effects. This may\nbe attributable to the time frame. The retrospective\nwindow at T1 may not capture the cumulative\neffects of comparative thinking over time, as the\nbereavement process is still in its early stages, and\ncomparative thinking in the last three weeks may\nnot be predictive of grief symptoms six months\nlater. Rumination was assessed at a more trait-like,\nhabitual level and may, thus, have exerted longerterm effects. Yet, in the concurrent models at T2,\nspecific comparisons in the last three weeks may\nbecome more representative of the individual’s cognitive processes. The\nfinding that it was negatively associated with\ndepression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms only\nconcurrently at T2 suggests that the immediate affective impact of well-being comparisons may have a\nmore direct and short-term influence on mental\nhealth than a lasting longitudinal effect that cumulates over six months (when regressed with more\ntrait like predictors). While rumination may also\namplify well-being comparisons or vice versa\n(Schlechter & Morina, 2023), as evidenced by their\nbidirectional associations, they remain distinct constructs with unique implications for grief. Brooding\nrumination, sometimes referred to as ‘moody pondering’ (Treynor, 2003), involves passively focusing\non distress and its causes or consequences without\nnecessarily engaging in explicit comparative thinking (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008). In the context\nof grief symptoms, brooding rumination may manifest as repetitive cognitions about the loss, its causes,\nor self-directed blame. In contrast, well-being comparisons involve evaluating (dis-)similarities\nbetween one’s current well-being and a standard,\nsuch as pre-loss functioning or the perceived wellbeing of others (Morina, 2021). While brooding\nrumination may sometimes include comparative\nelements (e.g. ‘Why am I struggling more than\nothers?’; Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991), well-being comparisons are explicitly focused on cognitive evaluations of potential well-being discrepancies.\nBrooding rumination perpetuates negative affect by\nfocusing on the loss, self-blame, or unresolved questions, potentially exacerbating grief reactions and\nrelated symptoms. In contrast, aversive well-being\ncomparisons may influence grief by accentuating\ndiscrepancies between one’s current state and\ndesired states, which can amplify feelings of inadequacy and distress and negatively influence coping\nefforts. Importantly, these processes might interact,\nwith aversive comparisons leading to negative\nmood and rumination. Conversely, rumination\n\n"}, {"pdf_page": 12, "text": "12\n\nP. Comparative thinking in the context of\nother predictors\nOur findings indicate that comparative thinking is an\nimportant factor to consider alongside the other predictors examined in this study. In line with prior\nwork, worry was consistently associated with outcome\nvariables, highlighting its role in maintaining anticipatory fears and catastrophic thoughts about one’s\ncapacity to recover from loss (McEvoy et al., 2013).\nRumination demonstrated even broader associations,\nsignificantly predicting grief, PTSD, depression,\nanxiety, somatic symptoms, and loneliness. This highlights how repetitive and uncontrollable focus on\nunresolved loss can maintain and increase distress\n(Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008). While avoidance\nshowed relatively limited effects compared to other\nvariables, its positive association with depression and\nPTSD at specific time points underscores its role as a\nmaladaptive coping strategy (Eisma & Stroebe,\n2017). In addition, avoidance was broadly assessed\nas experiential avoidance and may thus not accurately\ncapture disorder-specific aspects of avoidance. Childhood adversity emerged as a significant predictor of\nPTSD, depression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms\n(McLaughlin et al., 2012). However, its influence\ndiminished or was much smaller in the autoregressive\nmodels, suggesting that its effects may be partially\nmediated by more proximal factors.\nThe absence of consistent effects of comparative\nthinking on PTSD symptoms in the regression\nmodels, despite significant bivariate associations,\nsuggests that its relationship with PTSD may be better\nexplained through shared variance with other psychological factors, such as rumination or worry. PTSD is\ncharacterized by intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance\nbehaviour and hyperarousal, which may conceptually\nbe more strongly related with rumination and worry\n(Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008). These constructs\nmay mediate or overshadow the influence of comparative thinking when considered simultaneously. Importantly, PTSD symptoms in the present study were not\ndirectly related to the loss.\n5.5. Serial mediation effects\nOur longitudinal mediation models did not yield significant effects. This\nmay correspond with our discussion above concerning\nthe timeframe of comparative thinking. Effects of\ncomparison frequency on depression, anxiety, and\nsomatic symptoms were mediated through comparison discrepancy and comparison affective impact.\nThese findings suggest that well-being comparisons\nmay have an indirect influence on mental health outcomes through their cognitive (i.e. assessment of the\ncomparison discrepancy) and emotional (i.e. the\nimmediate affective reaction) impact. This is consistent with previous studies on well-being comparison\nthat repeatedly found this mediation in the context\nof various mental health symptoms (Morina &\nSchlechter, 2023; Schlechter & Morina, 2023). However, the serial mediation effects were significant at\nthe α = .05 level and did not meet our preregistered\nthreshold of α = .01, indicating that these pathways\nrequire further empirical validation, particularly\ngiven that post-hoc power analysis suggested the\nstudy was underpowered to detect these mediation\neffects.\n5.6. Theoretical contribution\nOur study contributes to the theoretical understanding of how aversive well-being comparisons may influence mental health outcomes in the context of\nbereavement. Our considerations were based on\ngComp (Morina, 2021), which highlights how comparison selection, perceived discrepancies, and affective and behavioural impact shape outcome\nvariables. Specifically, aversive well-being comparisons not only represent cognitive appraisals of wellbeing but also elicit affective reactions that intensify\ngrief-related symptoms. The dual process model\nemphasizes the need for individuals to balance two\ncomplementary processes when grieving: (1) lossoriented coping, which focuses on the emotional\naspects of the loss, and (2) restoration-oriented coping, which deals with the adaptation to life without\nthe deceased (Stroebe & Schut, 1999). Aversive wellbeing comparisons may influence both of these coping\nprocesses, and disrupt helpful oscillation as it mostly\nreflects loss-oriented coping processes. When individuals frequently engage in aversive well-being comparisons, it might exacerbate the loss-oriented aspect of\ngrief, as they become preoccupied with what they\nhave lost, hindering their ability to reorient toward\nlife outside of bereavement. Negative comparison\naffective impact may obstruct the process of restoration-oriented coping. From a cognitive behavioural\nperspective (Boelen et al., 2006), frequent engagement\nin aversive comparisons may evoke and maintain\ndepression, anxiety, and grief. Aversive well-being\ncomparisons may thus fuel maladaptive thinking\n\n"}, {"pdf_page": 13, "text": "EUROPEAN JOURNAL OF PSYCHOTRAUMATOLOGY\n\npatterns and reinforce cognitive distortions that the\ncomparer’s well-being is permanently diminished.\n5.7. Future research\nFuture research should further disentangle the role of\nwell-being comparison processes at multiple levels of\nanalysis, ranging from broader longitudinal trajectories to fine-grained within-person dynamics. In particular, micro-level approaches (e.g. ecological\nmomentary assessment) are needed to capture the\ntemporal sequencing and short-term fluctuations of\ncomparison frequency, discrepancy, and affective\nimpact in daily life (Schlechter et al., 2025, 2026).\nExperimental and intervention studies are also\nrequired to clarify causal mechanisms and determine\nwhether modifying maladaptive comparison processes\ncan reduce grief-related distress. Additionally, future\nwork should examine how well-being comparisons\noperate alongside established transdiagnostic processes such as rumination, worry, and avoidance,\nand whether they represent partially independent or\ninteracting mechanisms across different symptom\ndomains in bereaved individuals.\n5.8. Potential clinical implications\nOur findings suggest that aversive well-being comparisons contribute to distress in prolonged grief, underscoring their potential importance as a target for\ntherapeutic intervention. Integrating approaches to\nreduce comparison frequency, reframe comparison\ndiscrepancies, or improve coping with the emotional\neffects of comparisons could enhance the efficacy of\nexisting treatments for PGD. This aligns well with cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) interventions, which\nhas been found effective in treating PGD (LaPlante\net al., 2024) by targeting maladaptive thoughts and\nemotions. A targeted treatment focus on aversive\nwell-being comparisons may help patients balance\nloss-oriented and restoration-oriented stressors,\npotentially promoting better emotional processing\nand grief adjustment. However, these claims require\nvalidation through experimental and clinical research.\n5.9. Strengths and limitations\nThis preregistered study contributes to the literature\nby examining the role of aversive well-being comparisons in individuals experiencing loss. It expands upon\nprevious research by considering the impact of wellbeing comparison processes, including its frequency,\ndiscrepancy, and affective impact, and demonstrating\nits unique contribution to mental health outcomes\nbeyond established risk factors like rumination and\nworry. By investigating these factors alongside other\nkey transdiagnostic risk factors in bereaved\n\n13\n\nindividuals, our work provides valuable insights into\nthe complex cognitive processes that may intensify\ngrief and contribute to mental health problems.\nHowever, our study has some limitations. One\nnotable limitation is the dropout rate of 28%. While\nmental health variables were not associated with dropouts, unmeasured variables may have contributed to\nthe drop-out in our study and may thus have influenced\nthe results. Our study was limited to two time-points,\nsix months apart. Relevant short-term fluctuations\nand within-person person processes thus remain\nmasked in our study. Although our models tested mediational pathways, causality cannot be established.\nExperimental research is needed to address this limitation. Furthermore, we relied on a convenience sample\nfrom Prolific, which restricts the extent to which our\nfindings can be generalized. This concerns sample\ndiversity and recruitment. Although Prolific allows\naccess to a wide range of participants, participants\nwere self-selected and recruited online, which may\nintroduce selection biases (e.g. higher digital literacy,\ngreater willingness to participate in psychological\nresearch). In addition, the sample may not fully capture\nthe diversity of bereaved populations in terms of cultural background, socioeconomic status, or bereavement context (e.g. cause of death, relationship to the\ndeceased). Additionally, post-hoc power analyses\nsuggested that our study may not have had sufficient\nstatistical power to detect the mediation effects we\nhypothesized. This requires caution in interpreting the\nmediation findings and underscores the need for larger\nsamples in future research. Finally, childhood adversity\nwas assessed retrospectively and may be confounded\nwith mood-related recall bias (Dunn et al., 2018).\n\n6.
