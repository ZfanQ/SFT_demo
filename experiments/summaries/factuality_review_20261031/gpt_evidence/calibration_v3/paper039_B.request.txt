Discussion\nThis paper outlines the three-tiered online education\nand training programme developed to enhance professional knowledge and improve service support for\nUK women veterans, specifically those who have\nexperienced MST. Guided by KMb theory, which\nuses research evidence to inform real-world impact,\nthe programme formed part of a wider KMb initiative\nand was operationalized in line with the KTA framework (Field et al., 2014; Graham et al., 2006). A\nmixed-methods evaluation of attendee feedback across\nall three courses indicated that the programme successfully enhanced professional knowledge. The\ncourses were well received, 98 clinicians were trained\nin CPT, and the evidence-based service adaptations\nand considerations proposed in Course 2 were perceived to be practical, relevant, and implementable\nby those who completed the feedback survey. Positive\nfeedback on Course 2 prompted the research team to\nconvert the live sessions into self-guided materials,\nwhich remain available online alongside Course 1, to\nsupport sustained knowledge use and growing participation beyond the end of the current project.\n4.1. KMb frameworks and questions of impact\nThe KTA framework provided an effective scaffold\nthroughout the project, supporting the interrogation\nand operationalization of each stage, while maintaining a clear focus on outcomes. The framework linked\nthe early research evidence on UK women veterans\nthrough to the education programme. This process\ninvolved translating evidence into targeted problems\nand subsequent solutions while moving from knowledge creation to the action cycle. Stakeholder co-production also ensured that the programme was adapted\nand implemented in a manner appropriate to the context. However, notable limitations remain.\nPhipps et al. (2016) criticise the KTA framework\nbecause it does not explicitly define the end goal of\nthe KMb process. They argue that KMb is concerned\nwith sharing knowledge to ultimately support meaningful change and create impact beyond the mere creation of interventions. In their conceptualization,\nimpact can include social, economic, and health\nbenefits, and media and public awareness, as well as\naddressing specific vulnerabilities. Nonetheless, assessing and measuring what constitutes impact raises several issues. For example, the current study can be\nviewed as a patient-mediated intervention, as it aims\n\nto improve veteran health outcomes indirectly by\nchanging the knowledge and behaviour of health professionals (Stacey & Hill, 2013). However, interventions such as training and clinical capacity building\ncannot be viewed in isolation outside the context(s)\nin which they are ultimately delivered and actioned.\nThe professionals who engaged with the education\nprogramme worked across different organizations,\nstructures, and contexts; therefore, ongoing monitoring of whether new knowledge was embedded in\ntheir daily practice is challenging.\nRutter et al. (2017) argue that rather than concentrating on whether an intervention fixes a problem,\nthe focus should be on whether and how an intervention contributes to reshaping wider systems in a desirable manner. This more holistic approach promotes a\nmove towards continual KMb that ‘develops’ rather\nthan ‘builds’ capacity and knowledge by engaging participants at different levels: as individuals, groups, and\norganizations (Kislov et al., 2014). Viewed through\nthis lens, although participant numbers from Scotland\nwere comparatively low, at less than 10% of attendees\nacross Course 1 (n = 42) and Course 2 (n = 7), these\nattendees were predominantly engaged in policy and\nservice design roles at a time when services across\nthe nation were under review and redesign. As such,\nthe impact of the educational programme may be\ngreater than the numbers indicate, and the longerterm impact of the education programme in a Scottish\ncontext may yet be realized. The inclusion of international participants may also result in farther-reaching impact, once knowledge has been translated to\ntheir specific national contexts. However, assessing\nthis requires in-depth, longitudinal follow-up.\nFinally, the KTA framework underplays the role of\nso-called ‘knowledge brokers’, whose role is to promote the mobilization of evidence (Davies et al.,\n2015). In the current study, the research team acted\nas an initial broker, advocating for and demonstrating\nevidence-based practice, facilitating the building of\nprofessional networks through the live workshops,\nand recruiting participants. Notably, the decision to\ncontinue to make Course 1 and an adapted self-guided\nCourse 2 freely available beyond the stipulated end of\nthe project was made in response to requests from participants who were developing their own KMb projects and wanted to use the materials as signposts,\nthus assuming the role of brokers.\nHowever, comparatively few participants came\nfrom outside veteran-specific support organizations,\npotentially limiting the project’s wider impact. While\noutreach efforts were made, engagement from veteran\nemployers, for example, remained limited. This highlights a continuing gap in engagement and remains a\npriority area for future initiatives. Although successful\nknowledge brokering may have common features irrespective of context (Phipps et al., 2017), moving\n\n"}, {"pdf_page": 11, "text": "EUROPEAN JOURNAL OF PSYCHOTRAUMATOLOGY\n\nbeyond one’s own disciplinary boundaries into unfamiliar domains such as political or social policy can\nbe challenging (MacKillop et al., 2020). Doing so\nrequires clear guidance in the form of operationalizable KMb frameworks, potentially including more\novert benchmarking of project impact.\n4.2. Implementing and promoting change: CPT\nclinical capacity building\nCPT is a gold-standard treatment, and the structured\nprotocol requires the use of many clinical skills\nthroughout therapy (usually 12 sessions) (Resick\net al., 2024). Cohort 1 clinicians who completed CPT\ntraining reported significantly higher self-efficacy in\ndelivering CPT 3 months post-training compared to\npre-training. Five clinicians had applied CPT to treat\nPTSD related to MST in women veterans during this\nperiod. Although modest, these numbers reflect the\nearly stages of implementation within clinical practice.\nCPT was also used more broadly across clinicians’ varied caseloads, including with male veterans and other\nblue-light service populations, demonstrating wider\nclinical impacts.\nNotably, the ‘events in systems’ approach to KMb\nacknowledges that change requires not just the intervention itself, but also an appreciation that the context\nin which it is delivered may aid or hinder impact (Skivington et al., 2021). This approach includes wider,\ninterlinked questions around acceptability, implementational considerations, cost-effectiveness, and scalability. Importantly, positive impact is viewed as\nobtaining information that will be useful to aid\ndecision making and subsequent, future work. In\nline with this perspective, the current study collected\nqualitative data exploring clinicians’ experiences\nusing CPT, to generate context-sensitive insights\ninto barriers, facilitators, and implementation considerations beyond intervention effectiveness.\nClinicians acknowledged the effectiveness of CPT\nfor addressing feelings of shame and guilt (LoSavio\net al., 2023; Resick et al., 2002). Service system-level\nbarriers to implementation included limited opportunities to use CPT and resource limitations. However, those who had not yet had the chance to use\nCPT expressed their intention to do so in the future,\nunderscoring the importance of longer-term evaluation of evidence-based therapeutic implementation\nservice systems. For individuals who had not encountered service system-level barriers, clinicians’ confidence and perception of CPT, factors that are\nknown to shape implementation (Damschroder\net al., 2009; LoSavio et al., 2019), impacted its use.\nOngoing training and supervision were described as\nessential. This aligns with work by Pace et al.\n(2021), who found that post-training consultation\nand ongoing support improved clinicians’ self-\n\n11\n\nefficacy in delivering CPT, which was significantly\nassociated with implementation.\nAttendees reported actively disseminating CPT\nknowledge within their organization, which in some\ncases led to service system developments and the\naccommodation of CPT in clinical pathways. Six\nattendees held clinical lead roles, which limited their\nopportunities to directly implement CPT owing to\nsmaller caseloads and competing responsibilities, but\nalso enabled them to support other clinicians in delivering CPT. Notably, the second cohort of clinicians\ntrained included a higher number of individuals in\nsupervisory or lead roles. Training individuals in leadership positions has been shown to promote treatment fidelity (the degree of adherence to and\ncompetence in delivering a protocol) (Godley et al.,\n2011). The provision of such training in this study\nmay therefore be considered strategically valuable for\nincreasing service capacity, addressing barriers to\nimplementation (e.g. clinician confidence), and facilitating the organizational adoption of evidence-based\ninterventions.\n4.3. Strengths and limitations\nA key strength of this study is that co-production with\nwomen veterans was central to the project, to ensure\nthat the research processes and outputs were informed\nby lived experience, particularly given the limited evidence base outside the US context. The stakeholder\ngroup played a crucial role in informing the education\nprogramme and ensuring its relevance to the target\npopulation. Although evidence and action from international partners can be transferable, care was taken to\nadapt the programme’s content to the UK context.\nThe inclusion of both UK and international stakeholders represents a key strength of the programme;\nhowever, future similar initiatives should continue to\nensure that appropriate contextual adaptations are\nmade.\nThe choice to include true-or-false quizzes to\nconsolidate online learning materials in Course 1\nrepresents a limitation, as evidence suggests that\nalternative methods such as multiple choice may\nyield better learning outcomes (Hildenbrand &\nWiley, 2025). However, true-or-false options\nwere selected to support user engagement and minimize the time required to complete the online\ncourse materials. Future similar programmes\nmay wish to consider alternative consolidation\napproaches that more effectively balance efficiency\nwith retention.\nThe iterative nature of this work and the responsiveness to participant feedback have strengthened\nthe relevance and utility of the education programme.\nConsistent with the KTA framework, this adaptive\napproach supported the development of a self-guided\nversion of Course 2, and the expansion of available\nplaces on CPT training courses for UK clinicians. Eligibility criteria for the second cohort of CPT trainees\nwas broadened to include clinicians working with\nveterans of any gender, further increasing UK clinical\ncapacity in CPT. The inclusion of the factsheet, alongside the extension of training to clinicians working\nwith veterans of all genders, reflects a recognition\nthat MST can be experienced by any member of the\narmed forces, irrespective of age, rank, or gender,\nthus broadening the scope of the programme to\nenhance awareness for all individuals who may have\nexperienced MST.\nEvidence suggests that implementing new training\ninto existing services takes time, and embedding new\nknowledge or treatment modalities such as CPT\nwithin a service system, can take 6–12 months or\nlonger, suggesting that the 3 month follow-up period\nmay be insufficient to assess the long-term impacts\n\non clinical practice or to observe broader servicewide or policy-level changes (Cook et al., 2020; Wiltsey Stirman et al., 2017). Future research should\nexplore the impact of such programmes longitudinally\nto capture sustained impacts.\nMore widely, the authors are sensitive to the view\nthat, because the majority of participants were from\nclinical or support settings, the project could be considered translational research manifesting as continual\nprofessional development, rather than KMb, which\naims to cast a wider net of influence (Graham et al.,\n2006). Nonetheless, the efforts to include nonveteran-specific audiences, albeit limited in number,\nas well as different courses targeting specific groups,\ncan be viewed positively.\n\n5.
